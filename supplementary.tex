\subsection*{Reproducibility Notes}

All models were run with temperature set to zero where supported. Each API call used a fresh conversation context with no prior messages. The responses analyzed here were collected on 16--17 September 2026 (UTC) from model APIs only; no human participants were involved (see the Human Subjects statement in the main text). Replication code and derived data are released under the MIT License. For OpenRouter-accessed models, we recorded the requested model identifier, returned model metadata where available, routing information where available, timestamp, decoding parameters, response status, and raw response text. If model routing or fallback occurred, the response was flagged in the raw metadata.

Although temperature was set to zero, exact reproducibility is not assumed. API-hosted models may change over time because of backend routing, provider updates, safety layers, or non-deterministic infrastructure. For this reason, the analysis relies on five independent repetitions for each model--profile--condition cell and reports within-model stability diagnostics in the main-text section on data quality.

For the administrative-burden simulation, the maximum output length was set to \texttt{max\_tokens=500}. For the moral-foundations battery (MFQ-30), the maximum output length was set to \texttt{max\_tokens=2048}. Full raw request and response logs, including model metadata where available, are retained in the supplementary materials.

\section{Moral Foundations Questionnaire-30: Full Item Wording} \label{app:orientation-items} This appendix reports the complete 30-item Moral Foundations Questionnaire (MFQ-30) \citep{Graham2011} used to measure the five moral foundations described in Section~3.2, together with the two control items. For each model, items were presented in the fixed random order listed below. The instrument has two parts: sixteen relevance items (Part 1: ``When you decide whether something is right or wrong, to what extent are the following considerations relevant to your thinking?'') and sixteen judgment items (Part 2: ``Please indicate your agreement with the following statements.''). To match the response format of the policy-specific battery used in earlier versions of this study, we used a single 1--7 agreement/relevance scale (1 = not at all relevant / strongly disagree; 7 = extremely relevant / strongly agree) rather than the two-part 0--5 scale of the original MFQ-30; the instrument therefore measures the same five constructs with the same item wording but a uniform response metric. Items marked \textit{(R)} are reverse-coded before dimension averaging. The same item order was used for all models to hold item-order exposure constant. The scoring rule that turns the thirty ratings into the composite MFQ score is stated in the final subsection of this appendix.
\subsection*{Care/Harm (CA)}
\textbf{Relevance items.} Rate each consideration from 1 (not at all relevant) to 7 (extremely relevant).
\begin{enumerate}
\item[CA01] Whether or not someone suffered emotionally
\item[CA02] Whether or not someone cared for someone weak or vulnerable
\item[CA03] Whether or not someone was cruel
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Judgment items.} Rate each statement from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree).
\begin{enumerate}
\item[CA04] Compassion for those who are suffering is the most crucial virtue.
\item[CA05] One of the worst things a person could do is hurt a defenseless animal.
\item[CA06] It can never be right to kill a human being.
\end{enumerate}
\subsection*{Fairness/Reciprocity (FA)}
\textbf{Relevance items.} Rate each consideration from 1 (not at all relevant) to 7 (extremely relevant).
\begin{enumerate}
\item[FA07] Whether or not some people were treated differently than others
\item[FA08] Whether or not someone acted unfairly
\item[FA09] Whether or not someone was denied his or her rights
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Judgment items.} Rate each statement from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree).
\begin{enumerate}
\item[FA10] When the government makes laws, the number one principle should be ensuring that everyone is treated fairly.
\item[FA11] Justice is the most important requirement for a society.
\item[FA12] I think it's morally wrong that rich children inherit a lot of money while poor children inherit nothing.
\end{enumerate}
\subsection*{Ingroup/Loyalty (LO)}
\textbf{Relevance items.} Rate each consideration from 1 (not at all relevant) to 7 (extremely relevant).
\begin{enumerate}
\item[LO13] Whether or not someone's action showed love for his or her country
\item[LO14] Whether or not someone did something to betray his or her group
\item[LO15] Whether or not someone showed a lack of loyalty
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Judgment items.} Rate each statement from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree).
\begin{enumerate}
\item[LO16] I am proud of my country's history.
\item[LO17] People should be loyal to their family members, even when they have done something wrong.
\item[LO18] It is more important to be a team player than to express oneself.

\subsection*{Canonical Administration Protocol and Provenance}
The battery was administered to all fifteen model endpoints under a single frozen protocol in the canonical rerun, so the fifteen MFQ values are measured on one instrument rather than on a mix of protocols. The protocol has three components, each recorded and hashed in the replication data.
\paragraph{System prompt (verbatim).}
\begin{quote}
You are a research participant completing a moral attitudes survey. For each statement, respond with a single number from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Output only valid JSON.
\end{quote}
\begin{flushleft}
{\footnotesize SHA256 of the system prompt:}\\
{\footnotesize\ttfamily f57cc97029bbf0b054ffdd129912f875684ea99723ff445522a8d2ee752af820}
\end{flushleft}
\paragraph{User prompt.} The rendered user prompt opens with the two-part instruction (Part 1 relevance, Part 2 judgment, both on the uniform 1--7 scale), then lists the 32 items in the fixed order above as \texttt{R<order>. [<item\_id>] <item text>} and \texttt{J<order>. [<item\_id>] <item text>}, and closes with a request for a single JSON object mapping item ids to scores, e.g. \texttt{\{"CA01": 6, "FA02": 3, ...\}}.
\paragraph{Output schema and parsing.} Unlike the burden simulation, which requires a JSON object with a willingness score and a rationale, the battery uses a JSON object keyed by item id with integer values between 1 and 7. A response counts as fully parsed only if all 32 item ids are recovered with scores in range. All fifteen endpoints returned fully parsable responses in the canonical run; the two control items are excluded from scoring.
\paragraph{Provenance fields.} Each battery call is logged with the model identifier, provider, access route, the SHA256 of the system prompt and of the rendered user prompt, the raw response text, the parsed score vector, and a UTC timestamp; the protocol lock that fixes these strings is committed alongside the runner.
\subsection*{MFQ Scoring}
The composite is computed in three steps, all from the thirty item ratings and all on the same sample of model endpoints.
\textbf{1. Dimension scores.} Each foundation score is the unweighted mean of its six items (three relevance, three judgment):
\[
\mathrm{MFQ}_{m}^{(d)} = \frac{1}{6}\sum_{i=1}^{6} x_{m,i}^{(d)},
\]
where $x_{m,i}^{(d)}$ is model $m$'s rating on item $i$ of foundation $d$, on the 1--7 scale.
\textbf{2. Standardisation.} Each foundation score is standardised across endpoints:
\[
Z^{(d)}_m = \frac{\mathrm{MFQ}^{(d)}_m - \bar{x}^{(d)}}{s^{(d)}}.
\]
\textbf{3. Composite.} Following the standard individualizing-minus-binding convention in the moral-foundations literature \citep{Graham2011}, the composite is:
\[
\mathrm{MFQ}_m = Z^{(\mathrm{CA})}_m + Z^{(\mathrm{FA})}_m - Z^{(\mathrm{LO})}_m - Z^{(\mathrm{AU})}_m - Z^{(\mathrm{SA})}_m,
\]
where $\mathrm{CA}$ = Care/Harm, $\mathrm{FA}$ = Fairness/Reciprocity, $\mathrm{LO}$ = Ingroup/Loyalty, $\mathrm{AU}$ = Authority/Respect, and $\mathrm{SA}$ = Purity/Sanctity. Care and fairness (individualizing foundations) enter positively; loyalty, authority, and sanctity (binding foundations) enter negatively, encoding the standard direction in which each foundation is expected to relate to prosocial and policy judgments. The composite is \emph{not} rescaled after aggregation, so its standard deviation is not exactly one; in the fifteen-endpoint sample its standard deviation is 1.16.

\section{Narrative Coding Codebook}
\label{app:narrative-codebook}

This appendix reports the algorithmic coding protocol used to classify high-burden rationales into five interpretive frames. The unit of coding is the rationale (one to three sentences) associated with each model--profile--repetition observation under the high-burden condition. Each frame is coded as a binary indicator using regular-expression pattern matching. Frames are not mutually exclusive.

\subsection{Algorithmic Coding Procedure}

Rationales are coded using a rule-based algorithm that applies predefined keyword patterns (regular expressions) to each rationale text. The algorithm operates as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Text preprocessing:} Rationale text is converted to lowercase.
\item \textbf{Pattern matching:} For each of the five frames, the algorithm searches for predefined regular expression patterns (word boundaries applied where appropriate).
\item \textbf{Binary assignment:} A frame is coded as present (1) if at least one pattern matches; absent (0) otherwise.
\item \textbf{Multiple frames:} Because frames are not mutually exclusive, a single rationale may be coded as positive for multiple frames.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Pattern Dictionary}

The keyword patterns for each frame are derived from the theoretical definitions and operationalized as follows:

{\raggedright
\begin{description}
\item[Autonomy infringement:] \texttt{individual choice}, \texttt{personal choice}, \texttt{personal freedom}, \texttt{autonomy}, \texttt{privacy}, \texttt{consent}, \texttt{personal discretion}, \texttt{right to choose}, \texttt{individual liberty}, \texttt{procedural dignity}, and phrases indicating limits on personal autonomy.
\item[Procedural legitimacy:] \texttt{justified}, \texttt{safeguard}, \texttt{reasonable verification}, \texttt{necessary procedure}, \texttt{eligibility}, \texttt{fraud prevention}, \texttt{legitimate public need}, \texttt{due process}, \texttt{accountability}, \texttt{oversight}, and phrases indicating procedural justification.
\item[Collective responsibility:] \texttt{civic duty}, \texttt{protect others}, \texttt{communal benefit}, \texttt{vulnerable group}, \texttt{collective welfare}, \texttt{common good}, \texttt{herd immunity}, and phrases emphasizing community benefit. The earlier \texttt{public health} pattern was removed because it matched ``trust in public health (authorities)'' and ``public health center'' (the vaccination site), which express trust in authorities or location rather than collective responsibility.
\item[Access barriers:] \texttt{cannot afford}, \texttt{miss work}, \texttt{lose income}, \texttt{childcare}, \texttt{no regular doctor}, \texttt{lack of access}, \texttt{low income}, \texttt{unable to comply}, \texttt{disadvantage}, \texttt{single parent}, \texttt{hourly worker}, and phrases linking burdens to resource constraints.
\item[Coercive backlash:] \texttt{resentment}, \texttt{distrust}, \texttt{resistance}, \texttt{backlash}, \texttt{reduced legitimacy}, \texttt{mandate}, \texttt{penalty}, \texttt{enforcement}, \texttt{backfire}, \texttt{coercion}, and phrases indicating reactance to mandates.
\end{description}
}

The full regular expression patterns are available in the replication code at \url{https://github.com/yichao2022/value-sensitivity-llm-audit}, which is released under the MIT License (Copyright 2026 Yichao Jin); reuse and modification are permitted with attribution, and model outputs remain subject to the providers' terms of service.

\subsection{Manual Coding Audit}

The algorithmic coding was audited, not independently validated. A stratified random sample of 200 rationales (approximately 10\% of the 2,019 high-burden rationales, allocated proportionally across the fifteen endpoints) was inspected by the author. The audit procedure was:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Scope:} the auditor reviewed each rationale together with the algorithm's frame assignments, without access to model identity or predicted outcomes. The auditor was therefore \emph{not} blind to the algorithm's labels, and this is a single-coder quality check rather than an independent second coding.
\item \textbf{Definition check:} each positive algorithmic assignment was checked against the frame definitions in Table~\ref{tab:narrative-codebook}.
\item \textbf{Focus areas:} special attention was given to access-barrier coding, where generic mentions of paperwork or waiting time are not sufficient for a positive code under the algorithmic rules.
\item \textbf{Discrepancy review:} any rationale in which a pattern match appeared to violate a frame definition was flagged for review.
\end{enumerate}

The manual audit was conducted after the collective-responsibility pattern correction. The audit found that pattern assignments reflected the codebook definitions rather than superficial keyword matches, and no systematic misclassification pattern was identified that would require pattern revision.

Two limits follow from the audit design. First, because the auditor saw the algorithm's assignments and concentrated on positive ones, the audit cannot bound false negatives: a frame the algorithm missed may simply not have been noticed, and the audit yields no precision, recall, F1, or agreement statistic. Second, single-coder inspection is not independent of the algorithm it checks. The main text therefore describes the frame indicators as descriptive evidence of rationale--outcome co-expression rather than as validated measurements.

A blinded re-coding sheet is archived in the replication materials for a human-versus-rule validation of the coding: \path{outputs/h2_gold_coding_sheet.csv} contains the 200 sampled rationale texts with the frame labels, model identity, profile, repetition, and outcome withheld into a separate key file, and the sampling is reproducible from the canonical data with seed 20260917 (\path{prepare_h2_gold_sample.py}). Coding the sheet and running \path{score_h2_gold_coding.py} yields per-frame precision, recall, F1, exact agreement, and Cohen's \(\kappa\) against the rule-based assignments. Those statistics are not reported here because the sheet was not coded in this study; the audit above is what was actually performed.

\section{Additional Robustness Checks}
\label{app:additional-robustness}

This appendix reports additional robustness checks for the H1 orientation--effect analysis. The main H1 specification uses the MFQ composite (individualizing minus binding foundations, standardised across endpoints) as the model-level predictor of average burden effects. Because the composite combines five theoretically distinct moral foundations with fixed directional weights, we also examine whether the foundation-specific patterns persist and whether the composite null is stable across model composition.

\subsection{Caveats Qualified in the Main Text}

Three caveats qualify the null H1 result. First, the MFQ composite imposes structural assumptions---equal weighting across five foundations, fixed directional signs (individualizing positive, binding negative), and an additive structure---that may not match how value dimensions interact with simulated policy effects. Second, at $N = 15$ model endpoints, model-level regressions have limited power, which is why the power and minimum-detectable-effect diagnostics below are reported alongside the coefficient estimates. Third, the fifteen endpoints are a convenience sample of commercially available models, so the estimates are descriptive of this sample rather than high-powered population estimates.

\subsection{Expanded-Sample H1 Robustness}

Table~\ref{tab:h1_expanded_robustness} summarizes the key H1 robustness results for the original nine-model sample and the expanded fifteen-model sample. The MFQ composite does not predict model-level burden effects in either sample. In the original nine-model sample, the composite coefficient is positive and statistically indistinguishable from zero ($\hat{\beta}=4.55$, $p=0.172$, $R^2=0.248$). After expanding to fifteen endpoints, the coefficient remains statistically indistinguishable from a null effect ($\hat{\beta}=2.96$, 95\% CI [$-$2.17, 8.09], $p=0.235$, $R^2=0.107$). Re-standardising the dimensions within the nine-endpoint subset gives a smaller positive coefficient ($\hat{\beta}=1.92$, $p=0.151$, $R^2=0.270$); the slope is scale-dependent, so both standardisations are reported.

Table~\ref{tab:h1_dimension_checks} reports the complete set of foundation-specific regressions in the fifteen-endpoint sample: each of the five foundations is fitted separately. None of the five is individually significant (all $p \geq 0.095$). The two binding foundations show the largest point estimates in the theoretically expected (negative) direction---loyalty $\hat{\beta}=$ $-$4.73, $p=0.095$, $R^2=0.200$; authority $\hat{\beta}=$ $-$4.25, $p=0.138$, $R^2=0.161$---while the individualizing foundations are essentially flat (care $\hat{\beta}=$ $-$0.49, $p=0.870$; fairness $\hat{\beta}=0.34$, $p=0.909$). This directional pattern is consistent with the individualizing-minus-binding construction but does not reach conventional significance for any single foundation, so the dimension-level check does not overturn the composite null.

Two composite variants confirm that the null is not an artifact of the individualizing-minus-binding sign convention. An individualizing-only composite ($Z_{\mathrm{care}} + Z_{\mathrm{fairness}}$) has no association with burden effects ($\hat{\beta}=$ $-$0.04, $p=0.979$, $R^2=0.000$), and a leave-loyalty-out composite---dropping the strongest single foundation---remains non-significant ($\hat{\beta}=1.22$, $p=0.303$, $R^2=0.081$).

\subsection{Leave-One-Model-Out Diagnostics}

Leave-one-model-out diagnostics further support the main H1 conclusion. The composite MFQ coefficient is not a stable predictor of model-level burden effects. In the expanded sample, the bivariate MFQ association remains positive but weak and statistically indistinguishable from zero in every leave-one-model-out fit (slope range 0.72--3.79; all \(p > 0.10\)). Dropping Gemini 2.5 Pro shrinks the slope the most (0.72, \(p=0.824\)); dropping GPT-4.1 inflates it the most (3.79, \(p=0.125\)); neither fit reaches conventional significance.

These diagnostics reinforce the interpretation that H1 does not provide evidence for a stable aggregate orientation--effect relationship. The MFQ-30 is useful as a human-validated questionnaire used here as a theory-grounded diagnostic probe, but the expanded analysis does not support treating a scalar moral-foundations composite as a reliable predictor of simulated burden-effect magnitude across models.


\subsection{Leave-One-Profile-Out Diagnostics}

We re-estimated each of the three main models twenty-seven times, excluding one synthetic profile at a time (27 fits per model). Coefficient ranges across the 27 fits are archived with the replication package. The H1 MFQ composite slope stays between 2.5 and 3.4 and is never statistically distinguishable from zero ($p$ between 0.16 and 0.33), so the H1 null is not produced by any single test case. For H2 under the primary pair-fixed-effects specification, no retained frame is statistically distinguishable from zero in any of the 27 fits: the access-barrier coefficient is positive in 25 of 27 fits (range $-$0.09 to $+0.32$), the collective-responsibility coefficient ranges from $-$0.08 to $+0.01$, and the coercive-backlash coefficient is positive in all 27 fits ($+0.37$ to $+2.33$) but never significant. For H3, all three frame contrasts retain both sign and significance in all 27 fits (autonomy $+4.64$ to $+5.18$; collective obligation $-$11.01 to $-$10.70; equity/access $+4.56$ to $+5.10$; $p<0.001$ throughout).

\subsection{Power and Minimum Detectable Effect Diagnostics}
\label{app:h1-power}

The original nine-model H1 analysis was underpowered for small or moderate model-level associations. In that sample, the approximate 80\% minimum detectable slope for the composite MFQ index was \(\beta \approx 10.2\) (5.7 in the fifteen-endpoint sample). These thresholds are expressed on the standardised-composite scale used in the main analysis; they correspond to roughly 3.2 and 3.0 standard errors for the nine- and fifteen-endpoint samples, and the archived power simulation is included in the replication materials. The observed MFQ coefficient was far smaller than this threshold. Expanding the model sample to fifteen endpoints improves coverage but does not eliminate the basic limitation that H1 relies on model-level variation.

For this reason, the H1 estimates should be interpreted as descriptive diagnostics rather than high-powered population estimates. The expanded results are most informative as evidence against a large linear association between the composite MFQ index and simulated burden effects in this model sample. They should not be read as evidence that moral foundations are irrelevant to LLM behavior. Instead, the strongest evidence in the paper comes from the randomized within-model frame-manipulation experiment; H2 shows that naturally expressed rationale frames do not provide a robust within-pair diagnostic.
