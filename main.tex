\begin{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Objective:} To evaluate whether LLM-assisted policy simulations are robust to model choice, prompt framing, and measured policy-value orientations, and to characterize how sensitive simulated policy effects are to normative framing, using administrative burden in U.S. vaccination access as a testbed.

\noindent\textbf{Study Setting and Design:} Multi-level diagnostic framework. Fifteen LLM endpoints complete a policy-value orientation battery and simulate vaccination willingness for 27 standardized synthetic profiles under low- and high-burden conditions. We estimate composite orientation effects (H1), narrative frame associations (H2), and within-model frame manipulation effects (H3).

\noindent\textbf{Data Sources and Analytic Sample:} Data were collected on 16--17 September 2026 (UTC) from 15 large language model endpoints accessed through provider APIs and gateways. H1: 15 model endpoints; H2: 2,019 analyzable pairs; H3: 108 clusters (2,159 observations). Synthetic profiles (N=27) are constructed experimental stimuli, not human population samples; no human participants were involved.

\noindent\textbf{Principal Findings:} 
(1) Composite orientation--effect: PVOC coefficient $\beta_1 = $ $-$2.14, 95\% CI [$-$6.23, 1.95], $p = 0.28$---composite orientation does not predict cross-model variation.
(2) Narrative frame association: Access-barrier rationales associated with larger simulated effects ($\beta = 2.36$, 95\% CI [0.38, 4.33]); collective-responsibility rationales show no detectable association ($\beta = 0.69$, 95\% CI [$-$1.40, 2.79]); coercive-backlash rationales associated with larger effects ($\beta = 7.26$, 95\% CI [3.38, 11.15]).
(3) Within-model manipulation: Autonomy frames increase simulated burden effects by 4.96 points (95\% CI [3.57, 6.35]); collective-obligation frames reduce effects by 10.88 points (95\% CI [$-$12.54, $-$9.21]); equity/access frames increase effects by 4.84 points (95\% CI [3.45, 6.24]). Directions are model-specific: in one of four models the autonomy frame reverses sign.

\noindent\textbf{Conclusions:} LLM-assisted policy simulation is normatively frame-sensitive. The same model, profiles, and scenario can produce materially different estimates (pooled shifts of 4.8--10.9 points on a 0--100 scale, and up to 20.1 points in model-specific estimates) depending on analyst-selected framing. These estimates characterize LLM simulation behavior, not real-world vaccination responses.

\noindent\rule{\textwidth}{0.4pt}
\vspace{0.3em}

\noindent\textbf{What is known on this topic}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep,topsep=0pt]
\item LLMs are increasingly used to simulate social judgments and policy effects in health services research.
\item Prior work measures model values through direct survey prompts rather than downstream simulated policy effects.
\item Less is known about whether measured policy-value orientations shape simulated policy-effect estimates under policy conditions.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.3em}
\noindent\textbf{What this study found}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep,topsep=0pt]
\item Composite policy-value scores do not predict cross-model variation in simulated effects.
\item Narrative interpretive frames are systematically associated with simulated effect sizes.
\item Frame manipulation shifts simulated effects in every model tested: collective-obligation frames reduce them in all four models, while autonomy and equity frames increase them in three of four (one model reverses sign).
\end{itemize}

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Administrative burden in U.S. health services---the learning, psychological, and compliance costs that individuals face when interacting with public programs---is a well-documented barrier to care \citep{herd2018administrative,moynihan2015administrative}. Burden arises from forms, documentation requirements, waiting time, procedural uncertainty, and perceived loss of autonomy, and it matters most for people whose informational and material resources are already constrained \citep{herd2018administrative,baekgaard2021burden}. In U.S. health services research (HSR), administrative burden is treated as a determinant of access and use: patients report delayed or foregone care when they encounter administrative tasks they cannot complete, with the largest effects among those facing the greatest constraints \citep{Kyle2021,Shin2025}, and recent commentary in this journal calls for treating burden as a central rather than incidental feature of the U.S. health care system \citep{Shafer2025}. Systematic reviews document a large but fragmented empirical literature on how burden operates in citizen--state interactions \citep{halling2023administrative}. Understanding how administrative burden shapes health-service access is therefore a central question for HSR, where policymakers need tools to anticipate the likely effects of procedural changes before implementation.

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly explored as synthetic participants and behavioral simulators across social and behavioral research---as substitutes for survey respondents, as simulated agents, and as predictors of experimental outcomes \citep{Aher2022,Argyle2023,Park2023,Lin2024,Ziems2024,horton2023large,hewitt2024large}. Health applications have followed: LLMs have been used to generate synthetic health-related quality-of-life survey responses among U.S. adults \citep{Baek2026synthetic}, to simulate population behavioral responses to policy interventions \citep{koaik2026llm}, and simulated clinical encounters are now being audited for demographic bias \citep{Templin2025framework}. As these methods move from measuring opinions to predicting policy-relevant effects, a new methodological problem arises: seemingly technical simulated policy-effect estimates may depend on analyst-selected normative framing. The same model, same synthetic population, and same policy scenario can produce materially different simulated effects depending on which governance-value lens---autonomy, collective obligation, or equity---is activated in the prompt.

That LLM outputs respond to prompt wording and framing is already well established. Survey-based value and opinion measurements shift with response format, prompting technique, and question phrasing \citep{Rottger2024,Tjuatja2024,Faulborn2025,Kamal2025,Wright2024}, and political-scale scores move substantially across prompt variations within a single model \citep{Wright2024,Kamal2025}. What remains unclear is whether this sensitivity propagates to the quantities that policy analysts actually consume---simulated marginal effects of a policy condition, estimated within a specified model and scenario. The distinction is not cosmetic. For questionnaire-based value measurement, prompt sensitivity degrades the reliability of an instrument; for an HSR policy simulation, a shift of several points in a simulated administrative-burden effect changes the magnitude and the policy reading of the estimate being reported. Our contribution is therefore not to establish that LLM outputs are prompt-sensitive, but to relocate that finding from stated-opinion outputs to simulated policy-effect estimates in a health-policy setting, and to make it measurable through a diagnostic framework that separates aggregate orientation, expressed narrative frames, and experimentally assigned frames.

This paper develops and evaluates a multi-level diagnostic framework for auditing whether LLM-assisted policy simulation outputs are robust to alternative normative framings. We use administrative burden in U.S. vaccination access as a controlled HSR testbed to demonstrate the framework.

A central challenge is that model provenance is an inadequate proxy for value orientation. To avoid treating development origin as a fixed ideological category, we independently measure each model's policy-value orientation using a structured battery capturing five dimensions: autonomy protection, state trust, collective obligation, administrative-burden sensitivity, and coercion aversion.

The study addresses three research questions. First, do LLMs with different empirically measured policy-value orientations produce different simulated marginal effects of administrative burden on vaccination willingness? Second, are simulated burden effects systematically associated with the interpretive frames used in model rationales? Third, can simulated burden effects be shifted within the same base model by experimentally assigning governance-value frames?

Contributions: (1) shift LLM value-sensitivity study from opinion to simulated policy effects; (2) introduce a policy-value orientation battery; (3) provide a multi-level diagnostic framework for LLM simulation validity.

\section{Theoretical Framework and Hypotheses}


\subsection{Policy-Value Orientation}
\label{sec:orientation-effect}

Policy-value orientation is defined as systematic tendencies to interpret governance situations through assumptions about individual--institution relationships, legitimacy of public authority, and procedural costs. The five dimensions are anchored in established accounts of citizen--state relations: policy feedback and the social construction of target populations \citep{mettler2004policy,schneider1997social}, autonomy and self-determination \citep{ryan2017self}, reactance to perceived coercion \citep{powers2023reactance}, and the learning, psychological, and compliance costs emphasized in the administrative-burden literature \citep{herd2018administrative,moynihan2015administrative,kang2025development}. The PVOC aggregates these dimensions with theoretically specified weights, treated as a diagnostic aggregate rather than a validated scale. Internal consistency is mixed (only collective obligation reaches $\alpha = 0.869$), consistent with prior findings that human psychometric instruments do not transfer directly to LLMs \citep{Miotto2023,Li2025,santurkar2023whose}. Reported value and bias profiles also depend on how the instrument is administered: constrained versus open-ended response formats, prompting technique, and survey wording yield divergent measurements \citep{Rottger2024,Tjuatja2024,Faulborn2025}, and political-orientation estimates vary across model families and instruments \citep{Motoki2024,Rozado2024,Bang2024,Peng2026}.

\subsection{Three-Level Diagnostic Framework}
\label{sec:diagnostics}

The framework proceeds from coarse to fine-grained diagnostics. Each diagnostic is informative regardless of the others.

\textbf{Diagnostic 1 --- Aggregate Orientation.} We first test whether a scalar policy-value composite (PVOC) predicts cross-model variation in simulated burden effects. A null result would indicate that simple ideology/value scores are insufficient for auditing LLM simulation validity.

\textbf{Diagnostic 2 --- Naturally Expressed Framing.} We examine whether interpretive frames expressed in model rationales systematically co-occur with simulated burden effects. Access-barrier, autonomy-infringement, and coercive-backlash frames should correspond to larger effects; procedural-legitimacy and collective-responsibility frames to smaller effects. Because frames are generated rather than assigned, this diagnostic identifies rationale--outcome co-expression rather than causal pathways.

\textbf{Diagnostic 3 --- Experimental Frame Sensitivity.} We test whether experimentally assigned governance-value frames causally shift simulated burden effects within the same base model. Autonomy and equity/access frames should increase effects; collective-obligation frames should decrease them. Holding model, profiles, and scenarios fixed attributes any shift to framing itself.

Each diagnostic addresses a distinct aspect of normative-frame sensitivity and contributes to the overall assessment of simulation validity.

\section{Experimental Design and Methods}

\subsection{Model Sample}
\label{subsec:model-sample}

We evaluate fifteen LLM endpoints spanning nine provider ecosystems (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, Mistral, Cohere, Moonshot AI, DeepSeek, Qwen). The sample includes frontier proprietary, lower-cost, hosted open-weight, enterprise-oriented, and non-U.S. models. All models were run with temperature = 0 where supported; each cell repeated five times. All responses analyzed here were collected between 16 and 17 September 2026 (UTC). Detailed identifiers are reported in the Online Supplement.

\subsection{Policy-Value Orientation Battery}
\label{subsec:orientation-battery}

We administered a 20-item Likert battery measuring five dimensions: autonomy protection, state trust, collective obligation, administrative-burden sensitivity, and coercion aversion. Each dimension uses four items on a 1--7 scale. The Policy-Value Orientation Composite (PVOC) aggregates the five dimensions with theoretically specified weights, treated as a diagnostic aggregate rather than validated scale.

\subsection{Administrative-Burden Simulation}
\label{subsec:burden}

To measure each model's simulated burden effect, we designed a vaccination-access simulation as a controlled HSR testbed, in which the same standardized profile faces two policy conditions: low administrative burden and high administrative burden. The simulation was administered after the orientation battery but before any frame manipulation, using a fresh API call for each model--profile--condition--repetition.

\subsubsection{Standardized synthetic profiles (controlled test cases)}
\label{subsub:profiles}

We constructed 27 standardized synthetic profiles as controlled test cases that span theoretically relevant variation in income, work flexibility, and health-care access while holding age, gender, residence, education, family status, and trust in public health authorities constant. These profiles are not intended to represent a human population; they are experimental stimuli designed to isolate how models interpret the same administrative-burden scenarios under different model and frame conditions. The full profile set is reported in the Online Supplement.

\subsubsection{Low- and high-burden scenarios}
\label{subsub:scenarios}

Two burden conditions differ in administrative requirements: low-burden (online scheduling, nearby pharmacy, minimal documentation, 15 minutes) and high-burden (in-person registration, income verification, proof of residency, paper forms, 1--2 hour wait, one-week advance appointment). These scenarios serve as a \textbf{stylized testbed} for auditing LLM simulation validity, not as estimates of real-world burden magnitude. Full prompts are in the Online Supplement.

\subsubsection{Outcome: predicted willingness and marginal burden effect}
\label{subsub:burden-outcome}

For each model, profile, burden level, and repetition, we obtained a predicted vaccination willingness on a 0--100 scale. The model produced a structured JSON output containing an integer willingness score and a brief rationale. The primary outcome is the marginal effect of moving from low to high burden for a given model, profile, and repetition: \(\Delta_{mir} = W_{mi,\text{low},r} - W_{mi,\text{high},r}\). Positive values indicate that the model predicts a reduction in willingness under higher burden. For model-level analyses, we average across profiles and repetitions.

\subsection{Task-Compliance Diagnostics}
\label{subsec:capability}

Because comparable general benchmark scores are not uniformly available for all fifteen model endpoints, we do not construct a composite capability score or include capability-adjusted regressions. Instead, we assess whether each model complied with the simulation protocol using task-specific diagnostics computed from the study's own outputs: JSON validity, score-range compliance, non-refusal rate, repetition-level stability (within-profile--burden standard deviation across five repetitions), burden-effect dispersion (standard deviation of profile-level effects), and flip rate. These diagnostics, reported in Table~\ref{tab:data_quality}, verify protocol compliance and output stability rather than construct a general capability ranking. The substantive analyses therefore focus on measured policy-value orientation, predicted burden effects, narrative frames, and within-model frame responsiveness.

\subsection{Narrative coding framework}
\label{subsec:narrative-coding}

To test H2, we coded rationales using five theoretically derived frames: autonomy infringement, procedural legitimacy, collective responsibility, access barriers, and coercive backlash. A rule-based algorithm with predefined keyword patterns (regular expressions) coded each rationale; frames are non-mutually exclusive. Validation on 200 rationales (10\%) confirmed pattern validity. Binary frame indicators are used as descriptive evidence of rationale--outcome co-expression. Full patterns are in the Online Supplement.

\subsection{Within-Model Frame Manipulation}
\label{sec:frame-manipulation}

To test H3, we conducted a within-model experiment varying system-prompt frames while holding base model, profiles, scenarios, and output format fixed. This provides within-model evidence that framing influences simulated burden effects.

\subsubsection{Model selection}

The frame-manipulation experiment was conducted on a purposive four-model subset (GPT-4.1, Llama 3.1 70B Instruct, Qwen3.7 Plus, and Mistral Large) representing a frontier proprietary closed-source model, a hosted open-weight model, a non-U.S. model ecosystem, and a low-PVOC robustness case. This design tests whether frame sensitivity can be induced within diverse model endpoints, not the distribution of frame responsiveness across all fifteen models.

\subsubsection{Frame conditions and randomization}

Four system-prompt frames were used: \emph{neutral}, \emph{autonomy} (weighting individual choice, privacy, and procedural dignity), \emph{collective obligation} (weighting civic responsibility and public health coordination), and \emph{equity/access} (weighting unequal procedural costs and barriers faced by disadvantaged groups). The manipulation was fully crossed: each model evaluated every profile under each frame, with frame order randomized across runs.

\subsubsection{Statistical analysis}

To test whether the assigned frame shifts $\Delta$, we estimate the burden effect with model and profile fixed effects, and indicator variables for each value frame (neutral as the reference category), adjusting pairwise comparisons using the Holm--Bonferroni correction. Because the frame manipulation is fully crossed within each model, profile fixed effects are identified within-model and absorb all time-invariant profile-level heterogeneity. Standard errors are clustered at the model--profile level (108 clusters: 27 synthetic profiles $\times$ 4 base models) to account for non-independence of repeated queries within each model--profile unit. The inferential target is the model--profile--level frame contrast, not a population-level causal effect. Full results are reported in Section~\ref{subsubsec:results-h3} and the Online Supplement.
\subsection{Estimation Strategy}
\label{sec:estimation}

We test H1--H3 using hypothesis-specific estimation strategies. Because the main explanatory variables in H1 and H2 vary only at the model level, these analyses are interpreted cautiously: the primary emphasis is on coefficient direction, magnitude, confidence intervals, and robustness across alternative specifications rather than on conventional significance thresholds alone.

\subsubsection{Orientation-effect models (H1)}
\label{sec:est-h1}

The primary H1 analysis tests whether models with higher PVOC scores predict larger burden effects:
\[
\bar{\Delta}_m = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{PVOC}_m + \epsilon_m,
\]
where \(\bar{\Delta}_m\) is the average burden effect across profiles and repetitions. Because the identifying variation in PVOC exists only at the model level, this analysis is interpreted as a descriptive cross-model association rather than a high-powered population estimate. We also estimate separate bivariate regressions replacing the composite PVOC with each of the five orientation dimensions (autonomy protection, state trust, collective obligation, administrative-burden sensitivity, and coercion aversion) as diagnostic checks, not as confirmatory tests of validated subscales.

\subsubsection{Narrative frame-decomposition models (H2)}
\label{sec:est-h2}

H2 decomposes associations between PVOC, expressed frames, and predicted burden effects at the model--profile level. Estimates are interpreted as frame--outcome association diagnostics (not formal causal mediation because PVOC is measured, not randomized). Standard errors are clustered at the model--profile level; frame-specific \(p\)-values are adjusted using Benjamini--Hochberg FDR at \(q=0.10\).

\subsubsection{Frame-manipulation models (H3)}
\label{sec:est-h3}

H3 uses a two-way fixed-effects model: \(\Delta_{mcir} = \alpha_m + \mu_i + \sum \beta_k \mathbb{I}(c=k) + \varepsilon_{mcir}\), with neutral as reference. Pairwise comparisons use Holm--Bonferroni correction; standard errors are clustered at the model--profile level.

\subsubsection{Multiple-testing and robustness checks}
\label{sec:robustness}

Multiple-testing adjustments: Benjamini--Hochberg FDR at \(q=0.10\) for H2; Holm--Bonferroni for H3; none for H1 (single coefficient). Robustness checks include leave-one-profile-out, dimension-specific PVOC scores, unweighted PVOC, binary frame coding, and alternative willingness thresholds.

\subsection{Human Subjects and Institutional Review}
\label{sec:human-subjects}

This study did not involve human subjects. All analytic inputs are either synthetic profiles constructed by the author or text outputs generated by commercial large language model APIs in response to those profiles. No human participants were recruited, surveyed, interviewed, or observed, and no personally identifiable information or protected health information was accessed, collected, or analyzed. The work therefore does not constitute human-subjects research, institutional review board approval was not required, and no IRB approval or exemption number applies. All model outputs were obtained under the standard terms of service of the respective providers and access routes; the authors did not fine-tune, train, or otherwise modify any model using study inputs.

\subsection{Data and Code Availability}

Replication materials---the canonical simulation and frame-manipulation runners, the 20-item orientation battery and its scoring code, the rationale frame-coding patterns, the analysis scripts, and the derived response files---are available at \url{https://github.com/yichao2022/llm-policy-simulation}. The repository is released under the MIT License (Copyright 2026 Yichao Jin), which permits use, modification, and redistribution provided the copyright notice is retained; reuse of the prompt templates, the synthetic profiles, and the coded rationale indicators is covered by the same terms. \subsection{Hypothesis Tests}
\label{sec:results-hypotheses}

This subsection reports the hypothesis-specific tests as three independent diagnostic components consistent with the framework introduced earlier. H1 examines whether the model-level PVOC predicts cross-model variation in average administrative-burden effects. H2 examines whether narrative frames expressed in model rationales are systematically associated with predicted burden effects, independently of any aggregate orientation score. H3 examines whether experimentally assigned governance-value frames shift predicted burden effects within the same base model. The three tests are independently informative: each addresses a distinct level of evidence (aggregate orientation, narrative co-expression, and within-model causal frame), and each can contribute to the overall assessment whether or not the others yield significant results.

\subsubsection{H1: Orientation--Effect Models}
\label{subsubsec:results-h1}

\textbf{Diagnostic 1 Result: Aggregate orientation performs poorly.} The PVOC does not predict cross-model variation in simulated burden effects ($\hat{\beta}=$ $-$2.14, 95\% CI [$-$6.23, 1.95], $R^2=0.090$). At N=15, the minimum detectable $R^2$ is approximately 0.38, so the null result indicates that a simple scalar composite is insufficient for auditing LLM simulation validity. This is a valuable methodological finding: researchers should not rely on aggregate ideology/value scores alone to assess simulation robustness.

Figure~\ref{fig:pvc_delta} visualizes the bivariate relationship. Consistent with the regression result, the fitted line is shallow and the points are widely dispersed. Models with higher PVOC scores do not necessarily predict larger burden effects: GPT-4.1 has the highest PVOC score but produces one of the smallest burden effects ($\bar{\Delta}=25.63$), while Gemini 2.5 Pro has an essentially zero PVOC score and produces the largest effect in the sample ($\bar{\Delta}=53.93$).

The null H1 result should be interpreted in light of three limitations. First, the PVOC imposes strong theoretical assumptions---equal weighting across five dimensions, fixed directional signs, and an additive structure---that may not match how value dimensions interact with simulated policy effects. Second, as documented in Section~\ref{sec:orientation-effect}, the PVOC has limited internal consistency across its constituent dimensions, with only collective obligation reaching conventional reliability thresholds ($\alpha = 0.869$). A composite aggregate whose components do not cohere as a measurement scale cannot be expected to produce stable cross-model predictions. Third, at N = 15 model endpoints, model-level regressions have limited statistical power, making a null composite result difficult to interpret in isolation \citep{Rottger2024,Peng2026}. The null composite result therefore provides little evidence for a simple additive pathway and does not constitute evidence against normative-frame sensitivity through other, more granular channels. This limitation reinforces the value of the dimension-specific, narrative-level, and experimental evidence that follows.

Models vary considerably in both measured policy-value orientation and predicted burden effects, but the composite PVOC does not explain that cross-model variation. This negative finding is valuable: a scalar ideology/value score is insufficient to audit LLM simulation; frame-level diagnostics are more sensitive. The expanded sample strengthens the conclusion that simulated burden effects are not well captured by a single aggregate policy-value score; subsequent analyses examine whether more specific expressed frames and experimentally assigned governance-value lenses provide stronger evidence of normative-frame sensitivity in LLM-assisted policy simulation.

\subsubsection{H2: Narrative Frame-Decomposition Analysis}
\label{subsubsec:results-h2}

H2 examines whether interpretive frames in model rationales are associated with predicted administrative-burden effects, independently of any aggregate orientation index. The analysis is designed as a frame--outcome association decomposition: the goal is not to establish a causal pathway from PVOC to burden predictions, but to assess whether expressed rationale frames are systematically related to predicted burden effects in theoretically interpretable ways.

We first examined the prevalence of the five theoretically derived frames in the high-burden rationales. Three frames displayed meaningful variation and were retained in the expanded fifteen-model frame-decomposition analysis. Access-barrier reasoning appeared in 43.2\% of high-burden rationales after recoding the frame to require an explicit link between procedural costs and reduced access, resource constraints, work flexibility, childcare, provider access, or socioeconomic vulnerability. Collective-responsibility reasoning appeared in 75.2\% of rationales, while coercive-backlash reasoning appeared in 7.7\%. Autonomy infringement and procedural legitimacy showed little or no meaningful variation and were therefore reported descriptively but excluded from the main frame model.

Table~\ref{tab:h2_frame_decomposition} reports the retained frame--outcome estimates. Access-barrier reasoning is associated with larger predicted burden effects (\(\hat{b}=2.36\), \(p=0.020\)), indicating that rationales linking administrative requirements to concrete access constraints correspond to larger predicted reductions in willingness. Collective-responsibility reasoning shows no detectable association with burden effects (\(\hat{b}=0.69\), \(p=0.515\)): although it is the most common frame in the sample, rationales emphasizing civic duty or public health responsibility do not move predicted effects in a consistent direction. Coercive-backlash reasoning is also associated with larger burden effects (\(\hat{b}=7.26\), \(p<0.001\)), consistent with the idea that resistance or distrust frames amplify predicted burden sensitivity. Because each model--profile combination contributes five repeated runs, standard errors are clustered at the model--profile level to account for within-cell correlation.

Leave-one-model-out diagnostics support the stability of the frame--outcome associations: access-barrier reasoning remains positive in all fifteen leave-one-out samples (range $+1.51$ to $+3.81$) and coercive-backlash reasoning in all fifteen (range $+3.52$ to $+11.25$). Collective-responsibility reasoning is not sign-stable (positive in eleven of fifteen, range $-1.79$ to $+1.62$). In the frame-adjusted specification the PVOC term enters only as a model-level covariate; its coefficient is reported in the Online Supplement and is not interpreted as part of the H2 estimand, because PVOC is measured rather than randomized and varies only at the model level. Overall, H2 receives partial support: the evidence shows that model rationales contain interpretable frames that move systematically with simulated burden effects, consistent with value sensitivity through expressed narrative frames rather than a single aggregate orientation score.

\subsubsection{H3: Within-Model Frame Manipulation}
\label{subsubsec:results-h3}

H3 tests whether governance-value frames can shift predicted burden effects within the same base model, using a purposive four-model subset: GPT-4.1, Llama 3.1 70B Instruct, Qwen3.7 Plus, and Mistral Large.

Figure~\ref{fig:h3_forest} reports pooled frame effects, using the neutral frame as the reference category. The inferential unit is the model--profile--frame combination (108 clusters: 27 synthetic profiles $\times$ 4 base models), with standard errors clustered at the model--profile level to account for non-independence of repeated queries within each model--profile unit. The results support H3: the autonomy frame increases the predicted burden effect by 4.96 points (95\% CI [3.57, 6.35]); the collective-obligation frame decreases it by 10.88 points (95\% CI [-12.54, -9.21]); the equity/access frame increases it by 4.84 points (95\% CI [3.45, 6.24]). All three frame contrasts remain significant after Holm--Bonferroni adjustment. The pooled four-model specification explains substantial variation in burden effects ($N=2{,}159$ profile--repetition observations, 108 model--profile clusters, adjusted $R^2=0.77$). The pooled estimates are averages over heterogeneous model-level responses: the sign of the autonomy contrast is not uniform across the four models, and the model-specific results are reported below and in the Online Supplement.

These estimates should be interpreted as model--profile--level frame contrasts rather than population-level causal effects. The 2,159 observations are not independent patient-level observations; they represent 27 synthetic profiles $\times$ 4 frames $\times$ 4 models, with up to five repetitions per profile--frame--model cell. The clustering adjustment treats the model--profile unit as the primary sampling unit, reflecting the study design in which synthetic profiles are the controlled test cases and model queries are repeated measurements. The clustered standard errors (SE $=0.72$--$0.84$) are substantially larger than unclustered ones but remain small relative to the estimated coefficients, reflecting the within-model--profile consistency of LLM responses at temperature = 0 rather than population-level precision in the traditional sense. The effect sizes (4.96, 4.84, 10.88 points on a 0--100 willingness scale in the pooled specification, and up to 20.11 points in the model-specific estimates) are the primary quantities of interest, with uncertainty quantified via model--profile--clustered standard errors.

The pooled effects are directionally consistent with the theoretical expectations in three of the four models: the autonomy frame increases burden sensitivity by emphasizing individual choice, privacy, and procedural dignity; the collective-obligation frame reduces it by emphasizing civic responsibility and tolerance of inconvenience; and the equity/access frame increases it by emphasizing unequal procedural costs. The Mistral Large extension is especially informative: although Mistral Large has the lowest PVOC score among the four H3 models, all three frame manipulations shift its simulated burden effects in the expected directions. The exception is Qwen3.7 Plus, for which the autonomy frame \emph{reduces} the simulated burden effect ($-2.87$, 95\% CI $[-4.53, -1.22]$) and the equity/access frame has no detectable effect. Taken together, H3 provides the clearest evidence that governance-value framing can shape LLM-assisted policy simulation---the within-model manipulation moves simulated effects by up to 20 points on a 0--100 scale, with the collective-obligation contrast reducing effects in all four models---while also showing that the direction of frame responsiveness is model-specific rather than a general property of LLMs. A frame that amplifies burden sensitivity in one endpoint can attenuate it in another, so an audit that reports only pooled estimates would misstate the fragility of any single model's output.

\subsection{Robustness Checks}
\label{sec:results-robustness}

For H1, the expanded fifteen-model analysis reinforces the null result for the PVOC (\(\hat{\beta}=$-$2.14\), 95\% CI \([-6.23, 1.95]\), \(R^2=0.090\)). Dimension-specific checks also caution against overinterpreting the original nine-model results: the collective-obligation association is non-significant in both the nine- and fifteen-model samples and is best interpreted as a sample-sensitive exploratory pattern rather than a stable dimension-level predictor. For H2, leave-one-model-out diagnostics show stable coefficients for access-barrier reasoning (positive in all fifteen leave-one-out samples) and coercive-backlash reasoning (positive in all fifteen); the collective-responsibility coefficient is not sign-stable. For H3, model-specific frame regressions show the pooled manipulation is not driven by a single base model: the collective-obligation frame reduces predicted burden effects in all four models (significantly in three), and the autonomy frame is positive and significant in three of four. The equity/access frame is more heterogeneous (positive and significant for GPT-4.1, Llama 3.1 70B, and Mistral Large; no detectable effect for Qwen3.7 Plus, whose autonomy contrast is also negative). Detailed H1, H2, and H3 robustness results are reported in the Online Supplement.

Robustness checks support granular interpretation: simulated policy effects are more sensitive to interpretive frames and assigned governance-value frames than to a single aggregate PVOC score.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

This study develops and evaluates a three-level diagnostic framework for auditing normative-frame sensitivity in LLM-assisted policy simulation. Using administrative burden in vaccination access as a controlled HSR testbed, we demonstrate that seemingly technical simulated policy-effect estimates depend on analyst-selected normative framing.

\textbf{Summary of Diagnostic Results.} Diagnostic 1 shows that aggregate policy-value scores (PVOC) do not predict cross-model variation---a valuable negative finding indicating that simple scalar composites are insufficient for validity auditing. Diagnostic 2 demonstrates that naturally expressed interpretive frames systematically co-occur with simulated effects: access-barrier and coercive-backlash rationales correspond to larger burden effects, whereas collective-responsibility rationales show no detectable association. Diagnostic 3 confirms experimental frame sensitivity: holding model and population fixed, alternative governance-value frames (autonomy, collective obligation, equity/access) materially shift estimated policy effects---by 4.8 to 10.9 points in the pooled specification, and by up to 20.1 points in model-specific estimates, with the direction of the autonomy contrast differing across models.

Taken together, the three diagnostics establish that LLM-assisted policy simulation is normatively frame-sensitive. The same model, same synthetic profiles, and same policy scenario can produce materially different simulated policy-effect estimates depending on the normative lens activated by the prompt.

The H1 null result is robust to alternative constructions, indicating that value sensitivity is not well captured by a single scalar index.

The narrative analysis provides more stable rationale--outcome co-expression evidence. Access-barrier and coercive-backlash rationales are associated with larger predicted burden effects, and both remain sign-stable in leave-one-model-out diagnostics; collective-responsibility rationales show no detectable association and are not sign-stable. H2 should be understood as descriptive co-expression rather than causal mediation: willingness scores and rationales are generated simultaneously, so observed associations may reflect post-hoc justification rather than a causal mechanism.

The strongest evidence comes from the within-model frame manipulation. In a purposive four-model subset, assigned governance-value frames shifted simulated burden effects: the collective-obligation frame reduced effects in all four models, and the autonomy and equity/access frames increased effects in three of the four (one model reversed sign on autonomy and showed no detectable equity effect). This shows that LLM-assisted policy simulation is normatively frame-sensitive: the same model, profiles, and scenarios can produce materially different estimates depending on the normative lens activated by the prompt, and the direction of that sensitivity is itself model-specific.

The H3 frame-manipulation results may reflect instruction-following, but this is precisely the operational mechanism of frame sensitivity. H3 quantifies steerability under alternative governance-value frames---a swing of up to 20 points on a 0--100 scale---which is the fragility that simulation-validity audits are designed to detect.

\textbf{Relation to Prior Work.} Four comparisons situate these results. \emph{First, the findings align with and sharpen the synthetic-human literature.} Large-scale replication work shows that LLMs often recover the direction of experimental effects while systematically producing larger effect sizes than human studies, and that they return significant results at high rates where the original human studies found null effects \citep{Cui2025replication}; LLM replacement of human participants can harmfully flatten and misportray identity groups \citep{Wang2025identity}; and direct comparisons caution against treating synthetic respondents as survey substitutes \citep{bisbee2024synthetic}. Our frame manipulation identifies a specific upstream mechanism for such discrepancies in the policy-simulation setting: estimated effect magnitudes are partly a function of the governance-value frame the analyst activates, so replication rates and effect-size inflation are not properties of the model alone. \emph{Second, the failure of the scalar index is consistent with the measurement-instability literature.} If value measurement in LLMs is sensitive to format and phrasing \citep{Rottger2024,Tjuatja2024}, and if survey instruments do not behave as validated psychometric scales when administered to models \citep{Miotto2023,Li2025}, then a composite built by aggregating such items should not be expected to predict an external outcome. The null H1 result is therefore evidence about instrument validity, not evidence that value orientation is irrelevant. \emph{Third, H3 extends prompt sensitivity to a new object.} Prior work establishes format and phrasing sensitivity in opinion and scale measurements \citep{Wright2024,Tjuatja2024,Faulborn2025,Kamal2025}. We show the same sensitivity in simulated marginal effects of a health-policy condition, and that it survives a fully crossed within-model manipulation with model and profile fixed effects, so the shift is attributable to the assigned frame rather than to model choice or case mix. The resulting swing (up to 20 points on a 0--100 willingness scale) is comparable to or larger than the effects such simulations are asked to detect. \emph{Fourth, this matters more for HSR than for NLP evaluation.} In NLP, prompt sensitivity degrades the reliability of a measurement; it does not by itself yield a false statement about a population. In HSR, a frame-dependent simulated effect enters policy deliberation directly. Because administrative burden already delays or forecloses care for the patients least able to absorb procedural costs \citep{Kyle2021,Shin2025,Shafer2025}, a simulation that understates burden effects under one frame and overstates them under another is not merely imprecise---it is a mechanism for misdirecting program design. Health services researchers should therefore hold LLM-based simulation to the reporting standards applied to other modeling choices in the field.

The findings have three implications. First, audits should move beyond stated-opinion tests to examine how models simulate policy effects under different frames. Second, audits should measure specific dimensions and frame sensitivity rather than rely on coarse provenance labels. Third, prompt framing should be treated as part of the experimental design: simulations should not be reported as single neutral estimates when governance-value frames materially shift predicted effects. Analysts should report model identity, prompt framing, scenario wording, and robustness to alternative frames. Funders and journals could strengthen reporting standards by requiring prompt, model, and frame-sensitivity documentation for LLM-assisted policy simulations.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Research}

Six limitations qualify the findings. \textbf{(1) Model sample.} The cross-model analyses rest on fifteen commercially available endpoints, which are a convenience sample of the models available at the time of data collection rather than a random or systematically stratified sample of model families, sizes, or training regimes. At N = 15, model-level tests have low power (the approximate 80\% minimum detectable slope for the PVOC is 5.7 points), so H1 and the dimension-specific estimates should be read as descriptive diagnostics rather than high-powered population estimates. The PVOC is theoretically motivated but not yet psychometrically validated: internal consistency is adequate only for the collective-obligation dimension, and the collective-obligation association weakened once the sample was expanded, so the battery and its directional weights require validation across larger and more systematically sampled endpoint sets. In addition, H2 and H3 reuse repeated queries of the same synthetic profiles; standard errors are clustered at the model--profile level, but the resulting intervals reflect within-profile consistency of LLM responses rather than population-level precision, which is why effect sizes and their uncertainty intervals rather than $p$-values are the primary quantities of interest.

\textbf{(2) Synthetic testbed.} The twenty-seven standardized profiles and the vaccination-access scenario are controlled test cases, not a representative sample of U.S. vaccination programs or of the populations they serve. The income-verification and residency requirements in the high-burden condition are stylized procedural barriers typical of means-tested programs, not realistic requirements for vaccination access. The findings therefore speak to LLM-assisted policy simulation methodology---in particular, its sensitivity to normative framing---and not to the magnitude of administrative burden in real health service delivery or to the behavioral dynamics of vaccination uptake.

\textbf{(3) Prompt and protocol dependence.} Each diagnostic relies on a single analyst-chosen prompt per level, a fixed item order for the orientation battery, and temperature = 0. The reported magnitudes are therefore conditional on those design choices; we did not run a systematic sweep over prompt wording, role instructions, or item order, and the frame sensitivity documented here should be read as protocol-conditional rather than as a fixed property of the models. Applying the diagnostics therefore requires documenting the prompt and protocol under which the reported magnitudes were obtained.

\textbf{(4) H2 rationale coding.} Frame indicators for H2 are produced by rule-based coding of model-generated rationales, validated by manual inspection of a random sample of 200 rationales (approximately 10\% of the high-burden sample). The coding is coarse, the rationales are generated alongside the willingness score rather than elicited separately, and the resulting estimates are frame--outcome co-expression rather than causal mediation; observed associations may partly reflect post-hoc justification.

\textbf{(5) H3 treatment bundling.} The high-burden condition increases paperwork, documentation, waiting time, location friction, and scheduling delay simultaneously, so the estimated $\Delta$ reflects a multidimensional high-burden regime rather than any single component. Component-specific and interaction effects would require factorial designs that vary burden dimensions independently.

\textbf{(6) Absence of human calibration.} The framework contains no human ground truth. We can establish that simulated burden effects are sensitive to the assigned frame, but not whether the simulated magnitudes, or their frame sensitivity, correspond to those of human respondents. The diagnostics identify fragility, not validity of magnitude. Anchoring these simulations to externally calibrated human-subject data---for example, to trial-based or stated-preference benchmarks for the same policy scenario---is the natural next step, and would allow the frame-sensitivity results to be interpreted against a human reference point.

\subsection{Conclusion}

The central finding is that LLM-assisted policy simulation creates a new methodological problem for HSR: seemingly technical simulated policy-effect estimates may depend on analyst-selected normative framing. The three-level diagnostic framework demonstrates that (1) simple aggregate value scores are insufficient for validity auditing, (2) naturally expressed interpretive frames systematically co-occur with simulated effects, and (3) experimentally assigned governance-value frames materially shift estimated policy effects.

For HSR researchers using LLMs for policy simulation, the implication is clear: report frame sensitivity rather than treating one prompt/model output as a neutral point estimate. Analysts should document model identity, prompt framing, scenario wording, and robustness to alternative frames. The goal is not to eliminate values from policy simulation, but to make their influence measurable, transparent, and contestable---so that the method can be used responsibly as an HSR tool.
