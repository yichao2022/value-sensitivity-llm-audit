\begin{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Objective:} To test whether LLM-assisted policy simulations are robust to model choice, prompt framing, and measured policy-value orientations, using administrative burden in U.S. vaccination access as a testbed.

\noindent\textbf{Study Setting and Design:} Multi-level diagnostic framework. Fifteen LLM endpoints completed a policy-value orientation battery and simulated vaccination willingness for 27 standardized profiles under low- and high-burden conditions, yielding tests of composite orientation (H1), rationale-frame association (H2), and within-model frame manipulation (H3).

\noindent\textbf{Data Sources and Analytic Sample:} Responses were collected on 16--17 September 2026 (UTC) from 15 LLM endpoints accessed through provider APIs and gateways. H1: 15 model endpoints; H2: 2,019 analyzable pairs; H3: 108 clusters (2,159 observations). The 27 synthetic profiles are constructed test cases; no human participants were involved.

\noindent\textbf{Principal Findings:} 
(1) The composite orientation index does not predict cross-model variation ($\beta_1 = $ $-$2.14, 95\% CI [$-$6.23, 1.95]).
(2) With model and profile fixed effects, access-barrier rationales co-occur with larger simulated effects ($\beta = 1.08$ [0.24, 1.92]); collective-responsibility ($0.11$, $p = 0.88$) and coercive-backlash ($1.59$, $p = 0.17$) rationales show no detectable association.
(3) Assigned frames shift simulated burden effects within models: autonomy $+4.96$ [3.57, 6.35], collective obligation $-10.88$ [$-$12.54, $-$9.21], equity/access $+4.84$ [3.45, 6.24]; the autonomy direction reverses in one of four models.

\noindent\textbf{Conclusions:} LLM-assisted policy simulation is frame-sensitive: identical models, profiles, and scenarios yield materially different estimates (pooled shifts of 4.8--10.9 points on a 0--100 scale) depending on analyst-selected framing. These results characterize simulation behavior, not real-world vaccination response.

\noindent\textbf{What is known on this topic}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep,topsep=0pt]
\item LLMs are increasingly used to simulate social judgments and policy effects in health services research.
\item Model values are usually measured with survey prompts rather than downstream simulated policy effects, so whether those orientations shape simulated effects is unknown.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.3em}
\noindent\textbf{What this study found}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,noitemsep,topsep=0pt]
\item Composite policy-value scores do not predict cross-model variation.
\item Access-barrier rationales co-occur with larger simulated effects; the other two retained rationales do not.
\item Assigned frames shift simulated effects within models: collective obligation reduces them in all four tested models; autonomy and equity increase them in three.
\end{itemize}

\section{Introduction}

Administrative burden in U.S. health services---the learning, psychological, and compliance costs of interacting with public programs---is a well-documented barrier to care \citep{herd2018administrative,moynihan2015administrative}. It arises from forms, documentation, waiting time, procedural uncertainty, and perceived loss of autonomy, and it falls hardest on people whose informational and material resources are already constrained \citep{herd2018administrative,baekgaard2021burden,Kyle2021,Shin2025}. Policymakers therefore need tools that anticipate the access effects of procedural changes before those changes are implemented \citep{Shafer2025,halling2023administrative}.

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as synthetic participants and behavioral simulators---as substitutes for survey respondents, as simulated agents, and as predictors of experimental outcomes \citep{Aher2022,Argyle2023,Park2023,Lin2024,Ziems2024,horton2023large,hewitt2024large}---including in health applications such as synthetic quality-of-life survey responses and simulation of population responses to policy interventions \citep{Baek2026synthetic,koaik2026llm,Templin2025framework}. As these methods move from measuring opinions to predicting policy-relevant effects, a new methodological problem arises: seemingly technical simulated policy-effect estimates may depend on analyst-selected normative framing. If normative-frame sensitivity propagates to policy simulation, otherwise identical model--profile scenarios could yield different simulated effects under alternative governance-value frames.

That LLM outputs respond to prompt wording and framing is well established: survey-based value and opinion measurements shift with response format, prompting technique, and phrasing \citep{Rottger2024,Tjuatja2024,Faulborn2025,Kamal2025,Wright2024}. What remains unclear is whether this sensitivity propagates to the quantities policy analysts consume---simulated marginal effects of a policy condition. The distinction matters: for questionnaire-based value measurement, prompt sensitivity degrades an instrument; for an HSR simulation, a shift of several points in a simulated burden effect changes the policy reading of the estimate. Our contribution is to relocate this finding from stated opinions to simulated policy effects and to make it measurable through a framework that separates aggregate orientation, expressed rationale frames, and experimentally assigned frame clauses.

This paper develops and evaluates a multi-level diagnostic framework for auditing whether LLM-assisted policy simulation outputs are robust to alternative normative framings. We use administrative burden in U.S. vaccination access as a controlled HSR testbed to demonstrate the framework.

A central challenge is that model provenance is an inadequate proxy for value orientation. To avoid treating development origin as a fixed ideological category, we independently measure each model's policy-value orientation using a structured battery capturing five dimensions: autonomy protection, state trust, collective obligation, administrative-burden sensitivity, and coercion aversion.

The study addresses three research questions. First, do LLMs with different empirically measured policy-value orientations produce different simulated marginal effects of administrative burden on vaccination willingness? Second, are simulated burden effects systematically associated with the interpretive frames used in model rationales? Third, can simulated burden effects be shifted within the same base model by experimentally assigning governance-value frames?

Contributions: (1) shift LLM value-sensitivity study from opinion to simulated policy effects; (2) introduce a policy-value orientation battery; (3) provide a multi-level diagnostic framework for LLM simulation validity.

\section{Theoretical Framework and Hypotheses}


\subsection{Policy-Value Orientation}
\label{sec:orientation-effect}

Policy-value orientation is defined as systematic tendencies to interpret governance situations through assumptions about individual--institution relationships, legitimacy of public authority, and procedural costs. The five dimensions are anchored in established accounts of citizen--state relations: policy feedback and the social construction of target populations \citep{mettler2004policy,schneider1997social}, autonomy and self-determination \citep{ryan2017self}, reactance to perceived coercion \citep{powers2023reactance}, and the learning, psychological, and compliance costs emphasized in the administrative-burden literature \citep{herd2018administrative,moynihan2015administrative,kang2025development}. The PVOC aggregates these dimensions with theoretically specified weights, treated as a diagnostic aggregate rather than a validated scale. Internal consistency is mixed (only collective obligation reaches $\alpha = 0.869$), consistent with prior findings that human psychometric instruments do not transfer directly to LLMs \citep{Miotto2023,Li2025,santurkar2023whose}. Reported value and bias profiles also depend on how the instrument is administered: constrained versus open-ended response formats, prompting technique, and survey wording yield divergent measurements \citep{Rottger2024,Tjuatja2024,Faulborn2025}, and political-orientation estimates vary across model families and instruments \citep{Motoki2024,Rozado2024,Bang2024,Peng2026}.

\subsection{Three-Level Diagnostic Framework}
\label{sec:diagnostics}

The framework proceeds from coarse to fine-grained diagnostics. Each diagnostic is informative regardless of the others.

\textbf{Diagnostic 1 --- Aggregate Orientation.} We first test whether a scalar policy-value composite (PVOC) predicts cross-model variation in simulated burden effects. A null result would indicate that simple ideology/value scores are insufficient for auditing LLM simulation validity.

\textbf{Diagnostic 2 --- Naturally Expressed Framing.} We examine whether interpretive frames expressed in model rationales systematically co-occur with simulated burden effects. Access-barrier, autonomy-infringement, and coercive-backlash frames should correspond to larger effects; procedural-legitimacy and collective-responsibility frames to smaller effects. Because frames are generated rather than assigned, this diagnostic identifies rationale--outcome co-expression rather than causal pathways.

\textbf{Diagnostic 3 --- Experimental Frame Sensitivity.} We test whether an experimentally assigned governance-value prompt clause causally shifts simulated burden effects within the same base model. The manipulated object is the prompt clause itself: the causal claim therefore concerns the assigned framing of the prompt, not any claim about the values a model holds internally. Autonomy and equity/access frames should increase effects; collective-obligation frames should decrease them. Holding model, profiles, and scenarios fixed attributes any shift to framing itself.

Each diagnostic addresses a distinct aspect of normative-frame sensitivity and contributes to the overall assessment of simulation validity.

\section{Experimental Design and Methods}

\subsection{Model Sample}
\label{subsec:model-sample}

We evaluate fifteen LLM endpoints spanning nine provider ecosystems (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, Mistral, Cohere, Moonshot AI, DeepSeek, Qwen). The sample includes frontier proprietary, lower-cost, hosted open-weight, enterprise-oriented, and non-U.S. models. All models were run with temperature = 0 where supported; each cell repeated five times. All responses analyzed here were collected between 16 and 17 September 2026 (UTC). Detailed identifiers are reported in the Online Supplement.

\subsection{Policy-Value Orientation Battery}
\label{subsec:orientation-battery}

We administered a 20-item Likert battery measuring five dimensions: autonomy protection, state trust, collective obligation, administrative-burden sensitivity, and coercion aversion. Each dimension uses four items on a 1--7 scale. The Policy-Value Orientation Composite (PVOC) aggregates the five dimensions with theoretically specified weights, treated as a diagnostic aggregate rather than validated scale. All fifteen endpoints were administered the identical frozen battery protocol in the canonical rerun; the system prompt, item template, output schema, and their SHA256 hashes are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:orientation-items}.

\subsection{Administrative-Burden Simulation}
\label{subsec:burden}

To measure each model's simulated burden effect, we designed a vaccination-access simulation as a controlled HSR testbed, in which the same standardized profile faces two policy conditions: low administrative burden and high administrative burden. The simulation was administered after the orientation battery but before any frame manipulation, using a fresh API call for each model--profile--condition--repetition.

\subsubsection{Standardized synthetic profiles}
\label{subsub:profiles}

We constructed 27 standardized synthetic profiles as controlled test cases that cross income, work flexibility, and health-care access while holding age, gender, residence, education, family status, and trust in public health authorities constant. They are experimental stimuli designed to isolate how models interpret the same scenarios, not a representation of any human population; the full set is in the Online Supplement.

\subsubsection{Low- and high-burden scenarios}
\label{subsub:scenarios}

The two conditions differ only in administrative requirements: low burden (online scheduling, nearby pharmacy, minimal documentation, about 15 minutes) and high burden (in-person registration, income verification, proof of residency, paper forms, a 1--2 hour wait, one-week advance appointment). These are a \textbf{stylized testbed} for auditing simulation validity, not estimates of real-world burden magnitude.

\subsubsection{Outcome measure}
\label{subsub:burden-outcome}

For each model, profile, burden condition, and repetition we obtained a predicted willingness on a 0--100 scale from a structured JSON response (integer score plus a brief rationale). The primary outcome is the within-profile burden effect \(\Delta_{mir} = W_{mi,\text{low},r} - W_{mi,\text{high},r}\), positive when higher burden lowers predicted willingness; model-level analyses average across profiles and repetitions.

\subsection{Task-Compliance Diagnostics}
\label{subsec:capability}

Because comparable benchmark scores are not uniformly available for all fifteen endpoints, we do not construct a composite capability score. Instead we report task-specific diagnostics computed from the study's own outputs---JSON validity, score-range compliance, non-refusal, repetition-level stability, burden-effect dispersion, and flip rate (Table~\ref{tab:data_quality})---which verify protocol compliance rather than capability.

\subsection{Narrative coding framework}
\label{subsec:narrative-coding}

To test H2, rationales were coded with five theoretically derived frames (autonomy infringement, procedural legitimacy, collective responsibility, access barriers, coercive backlash) using a rule-based algorithm with predefined regular-expression patterns; frames are not mutually exclusive. Manual review of 200 rationales (about 10\% of the high-burden sample) identified no systematic coding errors, so the indicators are used as descriptive evidence of co-expression rather than as validated measurements. Patterns are in the Online Supplement.

\subsection{Within-Model Frame Manipulation}
\label{sec:frame-manipulation}

To test H3, we varied system-prompt frames within the same base model while holding profiles, scenarios, and output format fixed. Only the frame clause changes, so the experiment identifies the causal effect of assigned prompt framing on simulated burden effects within a model; it does not speak to the values a model is claimed to hold.

\subsubsection{Model selection}

The experiment used a purposive four-model subset (GPT-4.1, Llama 3.1 70B Instruct, Qwen3.7 Plus, Mistral Large) covering a frontier proprietary model, a hosted open-weight model, a non-U.S. ecosystem, and a low-PVOC case. This tests whether frame sensitivity can be induced across diverse endpoints, not its distribution over all fifteen.

\subsubsection{Frame conditions and randomization}

Four system-prompt frames were used: \emph{neutral}, \emph{autonomy}, \emph{collective obligation}, and \emph{equity/access} (weighting individual choice, civic responsibility, and unequal procedural costs, respectively). The manipulation was fully crossed---every model evaluated every profile under every frame---with frame order randomized across runs.

\subsubsection{Statistical analysis}

The frame effect is estimated with model and profile fixed effects and frame indicators (neutral as reference), with Holm--Bonferroni adjustment. Because the manipulation is fully crossed within models, profile fixed effects absorb time-invariant profile heterogeneity, and standard errors are clustered on the 108 model--profile units. The inferential target is the within-model--profile assigned-frame contrast; because only the prompt clause is manipulated, the estimate is the causal effect of assigned governance-value prompt framing rather than an effect of model-held values.
\subsection{Estimation Strategy}
\label{sec:estimation}

In H1 the PVOC varies only at the model level, so that analysis is descriptive; in H2 the frame indicators vary within models and profiles, so the model includes both sets of fixed effects.

\subsubsection{Orientation-effect models (H1)}
\label{sec:est-h1}

The primary H1 analysis tests whether models with higher PVOC scores predict larger burden effects:
\[
\bar{\Delta}_m = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{PVOC}_m + \epsilon_m,
\]
where the PVOC is standardized across models; because its variation is model-level, this is a descriptive cross-model association rather than a high-powered population estimate. We also fit each orientation dimension separately as a diagnostic check, not as a validated subscale.

\subsubsection{Narrative frame-decomposition models (H2)}
\label{sec:est-h2}

H2 estimates a frame--outcome association model with model and profile fixed effects:
\[
\Delta_{mir} = \alpha_m + \mu_i + \sum_{k} \beta_k \mathbb{I}(k \in r_{mir}) + \varepsilon_{mir},
\]
The frame indicators are rationale-level. Model and profile fixed effects absorb between-model differences in baseline burden effects and between-profile differences in case difficulty, so the coefficients are identified from within-model, within-profile co-variation. The PVOC does not enter the H2 model. Estimates are co-expression, not causal mediation, because willingness and rationales are generated in the same response; standard errors are clustered at the model--profile level and frame-specific \(p\)-values use Benjamini--Hochberg FDR at \(q=0.10\).

\subsubsection{Frame-manipulation models (H3)}
\label{sec:est-h3}

H3 uses a two-way fixed-effects model: \(\Delta_{mcir} = \alpha_m + \mu_i + \sum \beta_k \mathbb{I}(c=k) + \varepsilon_{mcir}\), with neutral as reference. Pairwise comparisons use Holm--Bonferroni correction; standard errors are clustered at the model--profile level.

\subsubsection{Multiple-testing and robustness checks}
\label{sec:robustness}

Multiple-testing adjustments: Benjamini--Hochberg FDR (\(q=0.10\)) for H2, Holm--Bonferroni for H3, none for H1 (single coefficient).

\subsection{Human Subjects and Institutional Review}
\label{sec:human-subjects}

This study did not involve human subjects: all analytic inputs are synthetic profiles constructed by the author and text outputs generated by commercial LLM APIs. No human participants were recruited or observed, and no identifiable or protected health information was accessed. The work therefore does not constitute human-subjects research, institutional review board approval was not required, and no IRB approval or exemption number applies. Model outputs were obtained under the providers' standard terms of service, and no model was fine-tuned or trained on study inputs.

\subsection{Data and Code Availability}

Replication materials---the simulation and frame-manipulation runners, the orientation battery and its scoring code, the frame-coding patterns, the analysis scripts, and the derived response files---are available at \url{https://github.com/yichao2022/llm-policy-simulation} under the MIT License (Copyright 2026 Yichao Jin), which permits use, modification, and redistribution with attribution. Raw model responses remain subject to the providers' terms of service. All reported results can be reproduced from the archived response files and committed scripts.

\subsection{Data Quality and Descriptive Statistics}
\label{sec:results-data-quality}

Table~\ref{tab:data_quality} reports task-compliance diagnostics for the expanded fifteen-model simulation (4,050 raw responses; 2,025 possible model--profile--repetition pairs, of which 2,019 are analyzable because six high-burden responses from Gemini 2.5 Flash failed structure checks). Mean predicted willingness varied substantially across models (47.99--65.48), motivating the use of the within-profile marginal burden-effect outcome in the main analyses.

\subsection{Hypothesis Tests}
\label{sec:results-hypotheses}

H1 tests whether the model-level PVOC predicts cross-model variation in average burden effects; H2, whether rationale-level frames co-vary with predicted effects; and H3, whether an assigned governance-value prompt clause causally shifts predicted effects within a model. The three tests are independent: each addresses a distinct level of evidence and is informative regardless of the others.

\subsubsection{H1: Orientation--Effect Models}
\label{subsubsec:results-h1}

\textbf{Diagnostic 1 Result: Aggregate orientation performs poorly.} The PVOC does not predict cross-model variation in simulated burden effects ($\hat{\beta}=$ $-$2.14, 95\% CI [$-$6.23, 1.95], $R^2=0.090$); at N = 15 the minimum detectable $R^2$ is approximately 0.38. A scalar ideology/value score is therefore insufficient to audit LLM simulation validity.

Figure~\ref{fig:pvc_delta} shows the same pattern: the fitted line is shallow, and high-PVOC models do not produce larger effects (GPT-4.1 has the highest PVOC score and one of the smallest burden effects, $\bar{\Delta}=25.63$; Gemini 2.5 Pro has an essentially zero PVOC score and the largest, $53.93$).

Three caveats qualify the null---the PVOC's equal weights and fixed directional signs, its limited internal consistency, and low model-level power---and are detailed in the Online Supplement; the null provides little evidence for a simple additive pathway without ruling out more granular channels of value sensitivity.

\subsubsection{H2: Narrative Frame-Decomposition Analysis}
\label{subsubsec:results-h2}

H2 asks whether interpretive frames in model rationales co-vary with predicted burden effects, independently of any aggregate orientation score. It is designed as a frame--outcome association analysis, not as a causal pathway from PVOC to burden predictions.

Three of the five frames varied meaningfully in the high-burden rationales: access-barrier reasoning in 43.2\% (after recoding to require an explicit link between procedural costs and reduced access, resource constraints, work flexibility, childcare, provider access, or socioeconomic vulnerability), collective-responsibility in 75.2\%, and coercive-backlash in 7.7\%. Autonomy infringement and procedural legitimacy were near zero and are reported descriptively only.

Table~\ref{tab:h2_frame_decomposition} reports the fixed-effects estimates. Only access-barrier reasoning is associated with larger predicted burden effects (\(\hat{b}=1.08\), 95\% CI \([0.24, 1.92]\), \(p=0.012\)), an association that survives the removal of between-model and between-profile variation. Collective-responsibility (\(\hat{b}=0.11\), \(p=0.876\)) and coercive-backlash (\(\hat{b}=1.59\), \(p=0.174\)) rationales show no detectable association; the larger coercive-backlash coefficient in a specification without fixed effects (7.27) reflects between-model differences rather than within-model co-expression.

Leave-one-out diagnostics support only this association: the access-barrier coefficient stays positive and significant in all fifteen leave-one-model-out fits ($+0.91$ to $+1.29$) and all twenty-seven leave-one-profile-out fits ($+0.89$ to $+1.26$), whereas the other two coefficients are never distinguishable from zero. H2 therefore receives limited support: frames and simulated effects do co-vary within models and profiles, but the pattern is confined to access-barrier rationales.

\subsubsection{H3: Within-Model Frame Manipulation}
\label{subsubsec:results-h3}

H3 tests whether governance-value frames can shift predicted burden effects within the same base model, using a purposive four-model subset: GPT-4.1, Llama 3.1 70B Instruct, Qwen3.7 Plus, and Mistral Large.

Figure~\ref{fig:h3_forest} reports pooled frame effects against the neutral frame, with standard errors clustered on the 108 model--profile units (27 profiles $\times$ 4 models; the 432 model--profile--frame cells are the observational units). All three contrasts remain significant after Holm--Bonferroni adjustment: autonomy increases the predicted burden effect by 4.96 points (95\% CI [3.57, 6.35]), collective obligation decreases it by 10.88 points ([-12.54, -9.21]), and equity/access increases it by 4.84 points ([3.45, 6.24]); adjusted $R^2 = 0.77$ ($N = 2{,}159$). Because the pooled estimates average heterogeneous model-level responses, the model-specific results are reported below and in the Online Supplement.

These are within-model--profile frame contrasts, not population-level causal effects: the observations are repeated queries of 27 constructed test cases, so the intervals reflect within-profile consistency of responses at temperature = 0 rather than population-level precision. Effect sizes (4.96, 4.84, and 10.88 points pooled, up to 20.11 points model-specifically) are the primary quantities of interest, with uncertainty quantified by clustered standard errors.

Because each model-specific regression rests on 27 profile clusters, inference was re-checked with a wild cluster bootstrap-$t$ and a finite-sample corrected covariance; no conclusion changes, including the marginal equity/access contrast for Mistral Large ($p=0.027$ bootstrap, $0.030$ corrected) and the autonomy reversal for Qwen3.7 Plus ($-2.87$, 95\% CI $[-4.53, -1.22]$; $p=0.002$). Pooled effects follow the theoretical expectations in three of the four models, and Mistral Large---the lowest-PVOC model in the subset---shifts in the expected direction on all three frames. H3 thus provides the clearest evidence that an assigned prompt clause shifts simulated effects within a model (by up to 20 points on a 0--100 scale, with collective obligation reducing effects in all four models), while the direction of that sensitivity is model-specific: a frame that amplifies burden sensitivity in one endpoint can attenuate it in another.

\subsection{Robustness Checks}
\label{sec:results-robustness}

Robustness checks leave every conclusion unchanged. The H1 null persists across dimension-specific and alternative constructions (the PVOC slope stays between $-2.26$ and $-2.07$ in leave-one-profile-out fits and never reaches conventional significance); the H2 access-barrier association is sign-stable in all fifteen leave-one-model-out and all twenty-seven leave-one-profile-out fits while the other frames remain null; and all three H3 contrasts retain sign and significance in every leave-one-profile-out fit, with model-specific regressions showing that the pooled manipulation is not driven by any single base model. Detailed results are reported in the Online Supplement.


\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

This study develops and evaluates a three-level diagnostic framework for auditing normative-frame sensitivity in LLM-assisted policy simulation. Using administrative burden in vaccination access as a controlled HSR testbed, we demonstrate that seemingly technical simulated policy-effect estimates depend on analyst-selected normative framing.

\textbf{Summary of Diagnostic Results.} Diagnostic 1 shows that aggregate policy-value scores do not predict cross-model variation. Diagnostic 2 provides narrower co-expression evidence: with model and profile fixed effects, access-barrier rationales co-occur with larger burden effects while collective-responsibility and coercive-backlash rationales do not. Diagnostic 3 shows that assigned frames shift estimated effects---by 4.8 to 10.9 points pooled and up to 20.1 points model-specifically, with the autonomy contrast differing in sign across models.

Taken together, the three diagnostics establish that LLM-assisted policy simulation is normatively frame-sensitive. The same model, same synthetic profiles, and same policy scenario can produce materially different simulated policy-effect estimates depending on the normative lens activated by the prompt.


The narrative analysis supplies within-model co-expression evidence: access-barrier rationales are associated with larger burden effects ($\hat{b}=1.08$, $p=0.012$), an association stable in all leave-one-out fits, while the other two frames show none. H2 is descriptive co-expression rather than causal mediation, because willingness and rationales are generated together and associations may reflect post-hoc justification.

The strongest evidence comes from the within-model manipulation. In the four-model subset, the collective-obligation frame reduced simulated burden effects in all four models and the autonomy and equity/access frames increased them in three of four (Qwen3.7 Plus reversed sign on autonomy and showed no equity effect). What is manipulated is the assigned prompt clause, so the estimate is its causal effect on simulated effects---a swing of up to 20 points on a 0--100 scale---and the direction of that sensitivity is itself model-specific.


\textbf{Relation to Prior Work.} These results sit at the intersection of three literatures. \emph{First, the synthetic-human literature:} large-scale replication work shows that LLMs often recover the direction of experimental effects while producing larger effect sizes and returning significant results where human studies found nulls \citep{Cui2025replication}, that LLM replacement of human participants can flatten and misportray identity groups \citep{Wang2025identity}, and that synthetic respondents are imperfect survey substitutes \citep{bisbee2024synthetic}. Our frame manipulation identifies an upstream source of such discrepancies: estimated magnitudes are partly a function of the frame the analyst activates, so effect-size inflation is not a property of the model alone. \emph{Second, the measurement-instability literature:} if value measurement is sensitive to format and phrasing \citep{Rottger2024,Tjuatja2024}, and if survey instruments do not behave as validated psychometric scales for models \citep{Miotto2023,Li2025}, then a composite built from such items should not be expected to predict an external outcome---the null H1 result is evidence about instrument validity, not evidence that value orientation is irrelevant. \emph{Third, prompt-sensitivity work:} prior studies document format and phrasing sensitivity in opinion and scale measurements \citep{Wright2024,Tjuatja2024,Faulborn2025,Kamal2025}; we extend it to simulated marginal effects of a health-policy condition and show that it survives a fully crossed within-model manipulation. This matters more for HSR than for NLP evaluation: in NLP, prompt sensitivity degrades measurement reliability, whereas in HSR a frame-dependent simulated effect enters policy deliberation directly. Because administrative burden already delays or forecloses care for the patients least able to absorb procedural costs \citep{Kyle2021,Shin2025,Shafer2025}, a simulation that understates burden effects under one frame and overstates them under another is not merely imprecise---it can misdirect program design.

The findings have three implications. First, audits should move beyond stated-opinion tests to examine how models simulate policy effects under different frames. Second, audits should measure specific dimensions and frame sensitivity rather than rely on coarse provenance labels. Third, prompt framing should be treated as part of the experimental design: simulations should not be reported as single neutral estimates when governance-value frames materially shift predicted effects. Analysts should report model identity, prompt framing, scenario wording, and robustness to alternative frames. Funders and journals could strengthen reporting standards by requiring prompt, model, and frame-sensitivity documentation for LLM-assisted policy simulations.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Research}

Six limitations qualify the findings. \textbf{(1) Model sample.} The cross-model analyses rest on fifteen commercially available endpoints, which are a convenience sample of the models available at the time of data collection rather than a random or systematically stratified sample of model families, sizes, or training regimes. At N = 15, model-level tests have low power (the approximate 80\% minimum detectable slope for the PVOC is 5.7 points), so H1 and the dimension-specific estimates should be read as descriptive diagnostics rather than high-powered population estimates. The PVOC is theoretically motivated but not yet psychometrically validated: internal consistency is adequate only for the collective-obligation dimension, and the collective-obligation association weakened once the sample was expanded, so the battery and its directional weights require validation across larger and more systematically sampled endpoint sets. In addition, H2 and H3 reuse repeated queries of the same synthetic profiles; standard errors are clustered at the model--profile level, but the resulting intervals reflect within-profile consistency of LLM responses rather than population-level precision, which is why effect sizes and their uncertainty intervals rather than $p$-values are the primary quantities of interest.

\textbf{(2) Synthetic testbed.} The twenty-seven standardized profiles and the vaccination-access scenario are controlled test cases, not a representative sample of U.S. vaccination programs or of the populations they serve. The income-verification and residency requirements in the high-burden condition are stylized procedural barriers typical of means-tested programs, not realistic requirements for vaccination access. The findings therefore speak to LLM-assisted policy simulation methodology---in particular, its sensitivity to normative framing---and not to the magnitude of administrative burden in real health service delivery or to the behavioral dynamics of vaccination uptake.

\textbf{(3) Prompt and protocol dependence.} Each diagnostic relies on a single analyst-chosen prompt per level, a fixed item order for the orientation battery, and temperature = 0. The reported magnitudes are therefore conditional on those design choices; we did not run a systematic sweep over prompt wording, role instructions, or item order, and the frame sensitivity documented here should be read as protocol-conditional rather than as a fixed property of the models. Applying the diagnostics therefore requires documenting the prompt and protocol under which the reported magnitudes were obtained.

\textbf{(4) H2 rationale coding.} Frame indicators for H2 are produced by rule-based coding of model-generated rationales, validated by manual inspection of a random sample of 200 rationales (approximately 10\% of the high-burden sample). The coding is coarse, the rationales are generated alongside the willingness score rather than elicited separately, and the resulting estimates are frame--outcome co-expression rather than causal mediation; observed associations may partly reflect post-hoc justification.

\textbf{(5) H3 treatment bundling.} The high-burden condition increases paperwork, documentation, waiting time, location friction, and scheduling delay simultaneously, so the estimated $\Delta$ reflects a multidimensional high-burden regime rather than any single component. Component-specific and interaction effects would require factorial designs that vary burden dimensions independently.

\textbf{(6) Absence of human calibration.} The framework contains no human ground truth. We can establish that simulated burden effects are sensitive to the assigned frame, but not whether the simulated magnitudes, or their frame sensitivity, correspond to those of human respondents. The diagnostics identify fragility, not validity of magnitude. Anchoring these simulations to externally calibrated human-subject data---for example, to trial-based or stated-preference benchmarks for the same policy scenario---is the natural next step, and would allow the frame-sensitivity results to be interpreted against a human reference point.

\subsection{Conclusion}

The central finding is that LLM-assisted policy simulation creates a new methodological problem for HSR: seemingly technical simulated policy-effect estimates may depend on analyst-selected normative framing. The three-level diagnostic framework demonstrates that (1) simple aggregate value scores are insufficient for validity auditing, (2) access-barrier rationales co-occur with larger simulated effects within models and profiles, while the other interpretive frames do not, and (3) experimentally assigned governance-value frames materially shift estimated policy effects.

For HSR researchers using LLMs for policy simulation, the implication is clear: report frame sensitivity rather than treating one prompt/model output as a neutral point estimate. Analysts should document model identity, prompt framing, scenario wording, and robustness to alternative frames. The goal is not to eliminate values from policy simulation, but to make their influence measurable, transparent, and contestable---so that the method can be used responsibly as an HSR tool.
